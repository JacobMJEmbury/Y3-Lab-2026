================================================================================
PART 1 - THE EXPERIMENT
================================================================================

1.1  The apparatus
------------------
  - An X-RAY TUBE with a copper (Cu) anode. A hot tungsten filament emits
    electrons; a high voltage U (35 kV here) accelerates them into the
    anode, which emits X-rays. The tube current I (mA) sets how many
    electrons arrive per second, and so how bright the X-rays are. It does
    not change their wavelengths.
  - A NaCl SINGLE CRYSTAL on a turntable (the goniometer). It reflects
    X-rays only at angles where Bragg's law holds (2.4), so it works as a
    wavelength filter.
  - A GEIGER-MULLER (GM) TUBE on an arm that turns at twice the crystal
    angle ("coupled" or "theta-2 theta" mode). It counts photons.
  - Software that steps the angle (0.1 deg per step here), counts for a
    fixed GATE TIME at each step, and stores the count RATE (counts per
    second) in an .xry file.

The angle the software reports is called beta in the raw files. In coupled
mode it is the crystal angle theta.

1.2  What was measured, and when
--------------------------------
All runs are at U = 35 kV and 0.1 deg per step. The current and gate time
of each run are stored in its .xry file and were read from there.

  Run (file)                       Day       I (mA)  gate   used for
  -------------------------------  --------  ------  -----  -----------------
  Cu_NaCl_long_1.2KPeak  (full)    Thursday  0.12    1 s    main spectrum
  1st_order_long         (n=1)     Thursday  0.12    10 s   n=1 peak
  2nd_Order_Good         (n=2)     Friday    1.0     10 s   n=2 peak
  3rd_Order_Long_Good    (n=3)     Friday    1.0     10 s   n=3 peak
  current scan                     -         0-1     10 s   dead time
  Test_run_long, column 4          Friday    0.1     10 s   zero offset
  normal / flipped crystal scans   Thu, Fri  various 1-10 s alignment
  unheated / heated full scans     Tuesday   1.0     1 s    temperature

History that matters:
  - TUESDAY's runs were taken BEFORE the angle scale was fixed. Their peaks
    sit about 0.12 deg higher than Thursday's. They are only ever used as
    heated-minus-unheated DIFFERENCES, never as absolute angles.
  - THURSDAY's runs were taken after the angle fix ("Angle_fixed" folder).
    Some Thursday runs named "Na_Cl_Cu_distance_*" were taken before the
    angle was set correctly and are not used (see the README in
    "Error analysis CSVs").
  - FRIDAY's normal-orientation scans agree with Thursday's to within
    0.003-0.007 deg, so the setup did not change overnight. That is what
    allows Thursday and Friday data to be combined.

    WHY this matters: the n=2 and n=3 close-up scans are Friday data, while
    the full scan and the n=1 scan are Thursday data. Without the
    Thursday-Friday agreement check you could not put them on one angle
    scale.

1.3  The two "checkmarks"
-------------------------
  Checkmark 1: fit the smooth bremsstrahlung background and find where it
               starts (lambda_min). That gives the tube voltage U.
  Checkmark 2: isolate the K-alpha peak in orders n = 1, 2, 3 and fit it,
               to find its position (so its wavelength) and to resolve the
               K-alpha1 / K-alpha2 doublet.


================================================================================
PART 2 - THE PHYSICS
================================================================================

2.1  Where the X-rays come from
-------------------------------
Two processes in the anode make X-rays:

  (a) BREMSSTRAHLUNG ("braking radiation"). An electron is deflected and
      slowed by a nucleus and radiates a photon. It can lose any amount of
      energy, from almost nothing up to all of it, so this gives a smooth,
      CONTINUOUS spectrum. It is the broad hump the peaks sit on.

  (b) CHARACTERISTIC LINES. An electron knocks a copper atom's innermost
      (K-shell, 1s) electron out. An outer electron drops into the hole and
      emits a photon whose energy is fixed by copper's energy levels:

          L -> K   gives K-alpha   (the strongest line)
          M -> K   gives K-beta    (weaker, shorter wavelength)

      K-alpha is really TWO lines very close together (a "doublet"):

          K-alpha1 : 2p(3/2) -> 1s   lambda = 154.06 pm
          K-alpha2 : 2p(1/2) -> 1s   lambda = 154.44 pm
          K-beta                     lambda = 139.22 pm

      The 2p(3/2) level has 4 quantum states and 2p(1/2) has 2, so
      K-alpha1 is about twice as intense: I(K-alpha2)/I(K-alpha1) ~ 0.5.
      The code uses 0.518, slightly above the simple 1/2 (see 5.9 for why
      the exact value hardly matters).

  Other lines seen: the filament is tungsten (W). Tungsten slowly
  evaporates onto the anode, so the spectrum also shows weak W L-lines. The
  two small "unidentified" peaks match them once the same ~0.24 deg angle
  offset as the Cu lines is allowed for:

          W L-alpha1  147.6 pm  Bragg 15.17 deg  seen at ~15.43 deg
          W L-beta1   128.2 pm  Bragg 13.14 deg  seen at ~13.41 deg

  (Cu K-beta: Bragg 14.29 deg, seen at 14.53 deg, the same ~0.24 deg
  offset.) These IDs are "most likely", not proven; they are not used in
  any result, only masked or cropped out.

2.2  The shortest wavelength: Duane-Hunt law
--------------------------------------------
An electron arrives with kinetic energy eU and cannot give a photon more
than that. The most energetic photon therefore has the SHORTEST wavelength:

    lambda_min = h c / (e U)

With hc = 1239.84 keV pm:

    U [kV] = 1239.84 / lambda_min [pm]

At 35 kV, lambda_min = 35.42 pm. Measuring where the bremsstrahlung starts
measures the tube voltage. This is Checkmark 1.

2.3  The shape of the bremsstrahlung: Kramers' law
--------------------------------------------------
Kramers' law gives the number of photons per unit wavelength:

    N(lambda)  proportional to  (lambda/lambda_min - 1) / lambda^2

It is zero at lambda_min, rises steeply, peaks, then falls slowly.

    WHY photons, not energy: Kramers' law is often written for ENERGY per
    unit wavelength, which has lambda^3 on the bottom. A GM tube counts
    photons, and each photon carries energy hc/lambda; dividing the energy
    form by that gives the lambda^2 form above. So lambda^2 is the right
    one here.

Real spectra fall below Kramers' law at long wavelength, because
long-wavelength X-rays are absorbed on the way: in the tube window, the
air, and the detector window. Photoelectric absorption grows roughly as
lambda^3, so the code multiplies by exp(-B (lambda/100 pm)^3).

    WHY call B "empirical": B also soaks up other smooth effects that the
    model does not include: the crystal's reflectivity changing with
    wavelength, the detector efficiency, and the fact that one 0.1 deg
    step covers a different width of wavelength at different angles
    (d lambda / d theta = 2 d cos theta). B is a fudge factor that makes
    the shape fit, not a measured absorption coefficient. Do not quote it
    as physics.

2.4  Turning angle into wavelength: Bragg's law
-----------------------------------------------
X-rays reflecting off successive planes of atoms (spacing d) add up in
phase only when the extra path, 2 d sin(theta), is a whole number of
wavelengths:

    n lambda = 2 d sin(theta)        n = 1, 2, 3, ...  (the "order")

  - NaCl has lattice constant a = 564.02 pm. The crystal face reflects off
    the (200) planes, spacing d = a/2 = 282.01 pm (d_NaCl in the code).
  - At each angle only wavelengths with n lambda = 2 d sin(theta) get
    through. Scanning theta therefore scans the spectrum.
  - The same line appears once in each order. Predicted Bragg angles:

          line          n=1      n=2      n=3
          K-alpha1      15.852   33.113   55.029   deg
          K-alpha2      15.892   33.205   55.231
          K-beta        14.290   29.582   47.775

  - Higher orders overlap: at any angle, lambda (n=1), lambda/2 (n=2) and
    lambda/3 (n=3) all satisfy Bragg's law. For the bremsstrahlung,
    lambda/2 only exists once lambda/2 > lambda_min, i.e. above ~7.05 deg.
    Below 7 deg the background is pure 1st order.

2.5  How far apart the doublet appears
--------------------------------------
Differentiate Bragg's law at fixed d:

    delta_theta = n delta_lambda / (2 d cos theta)       (radians)

With delta_lambda = 154.44 - 154.06 = 0.38 pm:

    n=1 : 0.040 deg   far smaller than the peak width: not resolvable
    n=2 : 0.092 deg   partly resolved
    n=3 : 0.202 deg   clearly resolved, a visible shoulder

Higher orders spread the lines further apart, because n grows and
cos(theta) shrinks. That is why n=3 is the place to study the doublet.

2.6  Why the peaks are Gaussian, and how wide
---------------------------------------------
The measured peaks have sigma ~0.07-0.14 deg. A line's own natural width
(Cu K-alpha1 ~2.3 eV out of 8048 eV) is only ~0.005 deg at n=1. So almost
all the width comes from the INSTRUMENT: slit widths, beam divergence, and
the crystal's mosaic spread. Many small independent blurring effects add
up to a Gaussian shape (central limit theorem). So a Gaussian is a sensible
peak model.

    Alternative not used: a Voigt profile (Gaussian convolved with the
    Lorentzian natural line shape). With the Lorentzian part 20x narrower
    than the Gaussian, it would make no measurable difference and add a
    parameter.

2.7  How an angle error becomes a wavelength error
--------------------------------------------------
From lambda = 2 d sin(theta)/n:

    delta_lambda / lambda = cot(theta) * delta_theta     (theta in radians)

    n=1  theta ~16 deg  cot = 3.46
    n=2  theta ~33 deg  cot = 1.52
    n=3  theta ~55 deg  cot = 0.70

The same angle error does FIVE times less damage to the wavelength at n=3
than at n=1. This is the single most important reason to prefer high
orders for wavelengths. The same formula, read the other way, says an
error in d moves the angle by -tan(theta) * (delta_d / d): high orders are
also the most sensitive to a change in d (used in 4.5).

2.8  Thermal expansion
----------------------
Warming the crystal by delta_T stretches d by

    delta_d / d = alpha * delta_T,      alpha(NaCl) ~ 40e-6 per K

which moves every peak to a slightly LOWER angle, by tan(theta) * delta_d/d.
Since tan(theta) grows with angle, real expansion shifts the n=3 peak about
five times more than the n=1 peak. That pattern is how 4.5 tells expansion
apart from a plain angle shift.

2.9  Dead time of a GM tube
---------------------------
After each count a GM tube is blind for a short time tau (~100-200 us)
while its gas discharge recovers. At high rates it misses photons, so the
measured rate R is below the true rate N. Two textbook models:

    NON-PARALYSABLE:  R = N / (1 + N tau)    <=>   N = R / (1 - R tau)
        a photon arriving during the dead time is simply lost
    PARALYSABLE:      R = N exp(-N tau)
        a photon arriving during the dead time is lost AND restarts it

GM tubes are usually treated as non-paralysable, and that is the model
used. At R = 1000/s and tau = 164.5 us, N = 1.20 R: a 20% correction. It
scales the peak HEIGHTS; it is symmetric about a peak's centre, so it
barely moves peak POSITIONS.


================================================================================
PART 3 - THE STATISTICS TOOLKIT
================================================================================

3.1  Counting (Poisson) errors
------------------------------
Photon arrivals are random. A count N fluctuates by sigma_N = sqrt(N). The
files store a RATE R = N/t (t = gate time), so

    sigma_R = sqrt(R t) / t

  - This is why the gate time matters: 10 s per step gives errors sqrt(10)
    = 3.2 times smaller (relative to the rate) than 1 s per step.
  - FLOOR OF ONE COUNT: a step that recorded 0 counts would get sigma = 0,
    i.e. infinite weight in a fit. The code uses max(R t, 1) counts. This
    is the usual fix; zero counts in a short gate do not mean "exactly zero
    with no doubt".
  - KEY POINT: the noise belongs to the RAW count, background included.
    Subtracting a smooth background curve does not remove any noise. A
    point that measured 20/s keeps the scatter of 20/s even if it reads ~0
    after subtraction. So errors are always computed from the raw counts,
    BEFORE anything is subtracted.

3.2  Propagating an error through a formula
-------------------------------------------
For y = f(x):  sigma_y = |df/dx| sigma_x. Used in the code for:

    dead-time correction  N = R/(1 - R tau)
                          sigma_N = sigma_R / (1 - R tau)^2
    tube voltage          U = 1239.84 / lambda_min
                          sigma_U = U sigma_lambda / lambda_min
    wavelength            lambda = 2 d sin(mu) / n
                          sigma_lambda = 2 d cos(mu) / n * sigma_mu  (mu in rad)

3.3  Least-squares fitting and chi-squared
------------------------------------------
A model f(x; p) has a few adjustable parameters p. Fitting chooses p to
minimise

    chi^2 = sum over points [ (y_i - f(x_i; p)) / sigma_i ]^2

  - Each point is weighted by 1/sigma_i^2: precise points count more. So
    the sigmas must be right, or the best-fit values shift.
  - REDUCED CHI-SQUARED  chi^2_nu = chi^2 / dof,  dof = points - parameters.
      ~1   : the model fits to within the error bars. Good.
      >>1  : the model misses something (or the error bars are too small).
      <<1  : the error bars are too big (or too few points to judge).
  - scipy's curve_fit returns the best-fit values popt and the covariance
    matrix pcov. sqrt(diagonal of pcov) are the parameter errors. The
    off-diagonal terms say how two parameters move together (3.6).

3.4  When to trust the error bars: the scaling rule
---------------------------------------------------
curve_fit has a switch, absolute_sigma:

    True   the sigmas are real uncertainties; parameter errors come
           straight from them.
    False  only the RELATIVE sizes of the sigmas matter. curve_fit
           rescales all parameter errors by sqrt(chi^2_nu), UP or DOWN.

The rule used in fits_copy.py (function weighted_fit) is a mix:

    trust the Poisson error bars (absolute_sigma=True), then
    if chi^2_nu > 1: scale the parameter errors UP by sqrt(chi^2_nu)
    if chi^2_nu < 1: leave them alone

    WHY up: chi^2_nu > 1 means the model does not describe the data to
    within the counting noise (the peak is not exactly Gaussian, the
    background model is approximate, ...). For the peak fits this was
    tested: the error bars are right and the cause is the peak shape
    (5.11, "Why are the reduced chi-squared values so high?"). The
    scatter about the model is then the honest measure of how well the
    parameters are known.
    WHY never down: the counting noise is a physical floor. A chi^2_nu
    below 1 with few points is usually luck, and shrinking the errors
    below Poisson would claim precision the data cannot have.
    WHY not absolute_sigma=False everywhere (what the code did before): it
    scaled errors DOWN for the n=1 free two-Gaussian fit (chi^2_nu = 0.88).
    It was originally chosen because the gate times were thought to be
    unknown. They are now read from the .xry files, so the error bars can
    be trusted.

deadtime.py and angle_offset.py still use curve_fit's default
(absolute_sigma=False). Their chi^2_nu are 5.7 and 26, both above 1, so
the result is the same as the rule above.

3.5  Statistical versus systematic errors
-----------------------------------------
  STATISTICAL (random): different for every point, shrinks as you collect
  more data. Example: Poisson noise. The fit handles it.

  SYSTEMATIC: the SAME for every point of a scan, does not shrink with more
  data. Example: if the angle scale reads 0.06 deg too high, EVERY angle is
  0.06 deg too high. More points do not help.

The trap: you could add a systematic error to every point's error bar. That
is WRONG. The fit would treat it as independent scatter, average it down by
roughly sqrt(number of points), and report an error far too small. It also
wrecks the chi^2.

3.6  The "variants" method for systematic errors
------------------------------------------------
The correct, simple way, used throughout:

    1. Run the whole analysis with every input at its best value
       ("nominal").
    2. Run it again with ONE uncertain input moved down by 1 sigma, and
       again moved up by 1 sigma, everything else unchanged.
    3. That input's systematic error on a result = half the difference
       between the "up" and "down" answers:

           sys = |result(+1 sigma) - result(-1 sigma)| / 2

    4. Repeat for each input. Add all errors in quadrature:

           total = sqrt( fit^2 + sys_1^2 + sys_2^2 + ... )

    WHY rerun the whole thing: the input may affect the result through
    several steps (e.g. the angle offset changes the bremsstrahlung fit,
    which changes the background subtracted under each peak, which changes
    the peak fit). Rerunning catches all of that automatically, with no
    derivatives to work out by hand.
    WHY half the up-down difference: for a small shift the result moves
    linearly, so half the full swing is the change per 1 sigma. It is just
    the numerical derivative times sigma.
    WHY quadrature: the sources (dead time, zero offset, alignment, ...)
    are physically independent, so their errors add like independent
    random variables. Quadrature is only right for INDEPENDENT errors;
    3.7 says what to do when they are not.

3.7  Correlated errors, and when errors cancel
----------------------------------------------
Two results that share the same systematic are CORRELATED: if the angle
scale is really 0.06 deg high, ALL fitted centres are 0.06 deg high
together.

  - In a DIFFERENCE of two angles from the same scan, a shared angle shift
    CANCELS. Example: the doublet separation mu2 - mu1. Its alignment,
    repeatability and zero-offset errors come out as exactly 0.
  - In an AVERAGE of results that share a systematic, that systematic does
    NOT average down. Averaging the n=1, 2, 3 wavelengths as if their errors
    were independent would understate the error. So quote the best single
    order (n=3) instead.
  - Within one fit, two parameters can be correlated too (e.g. mu1 and mu2
    in a two-Gaussian fit: if one moves right the other tends to move left
    to keep the sum in place). The error on mu2 - mu1 then needs the
    covariance:

        sigma(mu2 - mu1) = sqrt( var(mu1) + var(mu2) - 2 cov(mu1, mu2) )

3.8  Interpolation is not fitting
---------------------------------
  - An INTERPOLATING SPLINE passes exactly through every data point, noise
    included. It is good for DRAWING a smooth line through the data.
  - A FIT uses a physical model with few parameters and passes through the
    data "on average", weighted by the errors.

A spline evaluated at 2000 points holds no more information than the
points it passes through. Fitting a model to the spline instead of the data
invents 2000 fake "measurements", ignores the error bars, and makes the
answer depend on the interpolation method. So: splines for display only,
every fit to the measured points.

3.9  Gaussians: height versus area
----------------------------------
    g(x) = A exp( -(x - mu)^2 / (2 sigma^2) )

    A      peak HEIGHT          mu  centre          sigma  width
    FWHM = 2.355 sigma          AREA = A sigma sqrt(2 pi)

The AREA is the intensity (total photons in the line). Two Gaussians of
equal height but different widths have different intensities. A ratio of
intensities is a ratio of AREAS; it equals the ratio of heights only if
the widths are equal. This matters for the constrained fit (5.8).

Each section answers: what question, what data, what method, why that

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1  deadtime.py - how long is the GM tube blind after each count?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DATA: current_characterisation.csv. The goniometer was held at one angle
and the tube current stepped from 0.05 to 1 mA. The 0 mA row has no
reading and is dropped. 7 points.

IDEA: at fixed voltage the true photon rate is proportional to the current,
N = k I. The measured rate starts proportional too, then bends over and
saturates as dead-time losses grow (3202/s at 1 mA instead of ~7000/s).
The amount of bending gives tau.

METHOD: fit both models of 2.9 with N = k I. Free parameters k and tau.
Poisson weights sqrt(R/t) with t = 10 s (the rates have one decimal place).

RESULT:
    non-paralysable  tau = 164.5 +/- 5.0 us   chi^2_nu = 5.7   <- USED
    paralysable      tau = 109.9 +/- 2.1 us   chi^2_nu = 5.9
    linearised check tau = 179.5 us

CHOICES AND WHY
  - Non-paralysable is the standard model for GM tubes. Both models fit
    about equally badly (chi^2_nu ~ 6), so the data cannot choose. The
    effect of the choice: at ~1000/s the paralysable model corrects by
    x1.15 instead of x1.22. That changes peak HEIGHTS by ~6% but no angle,
    wavelength or voltage (dead time is symmetric about a peak).
  - A direct non-linear fit rather than the straight line 1/R = (1/k)(1/I)
    + tau. Taking 1/R distorts the error bars (the small rates at low
    current dominate) and the straight-line fit here is unweighted. It is
    printed and plotted only as a cross-check; it gives 179.5 us, in the
    same region.
  - chi^2_nu = 5.7 > 1, so the error on tau is scaled up by sqrt(5.7)
    (curve_fit's default does this). Possible causes: current not exactly
    proportional to the set value, or tube drift. The scaled error covers
    it.

HANDS TO fits_copy.py:  tau, tau_err (non-paralysable).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.2  angle_offset.py - where is the true zero of the angle scale?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DATA: Test_run_long_run4.csv (column 4 of Friday's Test_run_long.xry, 10 s
per step, 0.1 mA). Crystal removed; the detector swept through the direct
beam from -2.6 to +2.5 deg.

IDEA: with no crystal, the undeflected beam goes straight into the
detector, so the count rate peaks where the angle reads TRUE zero. If it
peaks at +0.062 deg, every reading is 0.062 deg too high:

    theta_true = theta_measured - zero_offset

METHOD:
  1. Dead-time correct the rates (the peak reaches ~2900/s, where the tube
     loses almost half its counts: N = 1.9 R).
  2. Fit a Gaussian plus a constant background. Floor of one count in
     10 s on the error bars.
  3. Repeat with tau -/+ 1 sigma (3.6) for the dead-time error on the
     centre.
  4. Compute the CENTROID (intensity-weighted mean angle) of the points
     within 3 widths of the centre, after removing the background. This
     uses no model.

RESULT:
    Gaussian centre        +0.0618 deg   (fit error 0.0025, dead time 0.0000)
    centroid               +0.0564 deg
    shape error            |difference| = 0.0054 deg
    zero_offset = +0.0618 +/- 0.0060 deg

CHOICES AND WHY
  - Dead-time correction first: dead time flattens the top of the peak.
    Without it a Gaussian cannot describe the shape (chi^2_nu 78 raw, 26
    corrected). Flattening is symmetric, so the centre barely moves
    (+0.0619 raw, +0.0618 corrected).
  - The shape error: chi^2_nu = 26 says the beam profile is NOT a
    Gaussian (probably closer to an image of the slits, with a flatter top
    and different tails). When the model is wrong, the centre depends on which
    model you pick. The Gaussian centre and the model-free centroid are
    two reasonable choices; their difference is a fair estimate of the
    shape uncertainty, and is added in quadrature. This more than doubles
    the error (0.0025 -> 0.0060 deg).
  - Gaussian centre as the central value rather than the centroid: the
    centroid depends on where you cut the tails (here 3 widths) and on the
    background level; the fit uses all the points. Either would do; the
    difference is already in the error.

WATCH OUT (important, unresolved): the direct beam locates the zero of the
DETECTOR arm. The Bragg peaks are set mainly by the CRYSTAL angle, and the
crystal's own zero (is its face exactly along the beam at theta = 0?)
cannot be seen with the crystal removed. Whether the 0.062 deg applies
fully to theta, or half of it (if the scan's angle was the detector angle
2 theta), depends on the scan mode, which the CSV does not record. See
Part 9, item 1.

HANDS TO fits_copy.py:  zero_offset, zero_offset_err.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.3  build_error_csvs.py - peak tables for the alignment and temperature runs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Lives in "Error analysis CSVs". Reads the raw .xry files directly, so it
also reads each run's current, gate time and voltage. Writes:

    alignment_full_scans.csv, alignment_friday_normal_1st_order.csv,
    temperature_full_scans.csv          the rates with Poisson errors
    alignment_peak_positions.csv,
    temperature_peak_positions.csv      fitted centre, height, FWHM, chi^2
                                        and shift of every line in every run
    runs_used.csv                       which file and column each run is

METHOD for each line in each run:
  1. Find the highest point inside the line's search window.
  2. Fit a Gaussian plus a straight-line background to +/-0.4 deg around it.
  3. If chi^2_nu > 1, scale the centre's error up by sqrt(chi^2_nu) (the
     same rule as 3.4).
  4. Reject the fit if the peak is too close to the edge of the scan, the
     centre wanders outside the window, or the height is below 3 sigma.
  5. Shift = this run's centre minus the reference run's centre; its error
     = both centre errors in quadrature.

CHOICES AND WHY
  - Local Gaussian + sloping line over +/-0.4 deg: these runs are full
    scans with many lines; a small window around each peak with a local
    straight baseline is enough to find the CENTRE, which is all these
    files are for. No bremsstrahlung model is needed.
  - Raw rates, no dead-time correction: dead time is symmetric about a
    peak, so centres are unaffected. Consequence: the HEIGHTS (and the
    "intensity per mA" ratios printed in 4.4) are distorted, because dead
    time squashes high rates more than low ones. Treat those ratios as
    rough.
  - K-alpha at n=1 at 0.6 mA has chi^2_nu of 30-300: the peak is squashed
    by dead time and is a doublet. The sqrt(chi^2_nu) scaling inflates its
    error accordingly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.4  alignment_error.py - how much does crystal alignment move the peaks?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTION: the crystal sits in a holder. If it is slightly tilted, every
peak appears at the wrong angle. How big could that error be?

DATA (from 4.3): peak centres in
    normal orientation:  Thursday 0.6 mA (REFERENCE), Thursday 0.12 mA,
                         Friday 0.12 mA, Friday 0.6 mA
    flipped about the ROLL axis (Friday), flipped about the YAW axis (Friday)
Only the Cu K-alpha / K-beta lines; the tungsten side peaks are left out.

PART A - REPEATABILITY. How much does the same peak move between repeat
normal scans when nothing is deliberately changed? Sample standard
deviation of each line's centre over the 2-4 normal runs, then POOLED over
all lines:

    pooled sigma_repeat = sqrt( sum of (runs-1) * std^2  /  sum of (runs-1) )
                        = 0.0143 deg   (7 degrees of freedom)

    WHY pooled: each line has only 2-4 runs. A standard deviation from 2
    values is itself uncertain by ~70%. Pooling assumes all lines share
    the same repeatability (reasonable: it is the same goniometer) and
    gives a much steadier number. The per-line values are printed for
    comparison (0.005-0.032 deg; the 0.032 is n=3 K-alpha from weak 1 s
    scans, fit errors ~0.025 deg, so mostly noise).

PART B - ALIGNMENT SHIFT. Shift of each line in the flipped scans relative
to the reference. Fitted with

    shift = a + b tan(theta)

  a = a constant angle shift (tilt, zero-type error)
  b = a change that grows like tan(theta), i.e. looks like a change in d

    roll:  a = -0.1274 +/- 0.0171 deg,  b = -0.0004 +/- 0.0429 (0.0 sigma)
           constant only: a = -0.1275 +/- 0.0062 deg, chi^2_nu = 0.34
    yaw:   only 2 lines found; constant only a = -0.0665 +/- 0.0150 deg

The roll shift is the SAME for every line (b is zero) and 13-23x each
line's own repeatability (about 9x the pooled value): a real, constant
tilt effect.

    sigma_align = |a_roll| / 2 = 0.0637 deg

CHOICES AND WHY
  - HALF the shift: if flipping the crystal reverses the misalignment,
    normal = true + e and flipped = true - e, so the shift is 2e and the
    true centre lies MIDWAY. The normal scan is then off by e = half the
    shift.
  - Used as an UNCERTAINTY (+/-), not a correction. Two reasons:
     (1) we do not know the flip reverses the error exactly;
     (2) the evidence is contradictory. Applying it as a correction (all
         angles 0.064 deg lower) brings the K-alpha1 peaks closer to
         Bragg (+0.16 -> +0.10 deg) but pushes U from 35.83 to 36.47 kV,
         further from the 35 kV set on the tube. Moving the other way does
         the reverse (U 35.21 kV, K-alpha1 off by +0.23 deg). No single
         constant shift fixes both, so it is honest to carry it as an
         error rather than pretend to know the true value.
  - ROLL, not yaw: the roll-flipped crystal still gave strong peaks (both
    orders, 3 lines) with a constant shift. After the yaw flip the
    intensity fell ~10x relative to the roll flip and only 2 lines could
    be fitted: the crystal was badly misaligned, so yaw is a worst case,
    not a typical error. It is printed as a cross-check only.
  - One CONSTANT value for every line: b = 0 means the error does not
    grow with angle, so the same 0.0637 deg applies at 3.6 deg (the
    bremsstrahlung onset) and at 55 deg (n=3), where no flipped scan was
    taken. This is an extrapolation and is stated as such.

HANDS TO fits_copy.py:  sigma_align_constant = 0.0637 deg,
                        sigma_repeat_pooled  = 0.0143 deg.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.5  temperature_error.py - does temperature change d?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUESTION: d = 282.01 pm is the value at room temperature. If the crystal
was warmer or cooler, d was different, and every wavelength from Bragg's
law is off by the same fraction.

DATA (from 4.3): Tuesday's unheated and heated full scans. Tuesday is
before the angle fix, so only the heated-minus-unheated SHIFT of each line
is used; any constant offset common to both runs cancels.

METHOD: 2.8 says expansion shifts each line by -tan(theta) delta_d/d, while
an angle-scale drift between the two runs shifts every line equally.
Fit the five shifts with

    shift = delta_beta_0 - tan(theta) * delta_d/d

and with each term alone:

    both terms        delta_beta_0 = -0.0147 +/- 0.0063 deg,
                      delta_d/d = (-1.0 +/- 2.8)e-4,  chi^2_nu = 0.09
    constant only     delta_beta_0 = -0.0127 +/- 0.0030 deg, chi^2_nu = 0.10
    expansion only    delta_d/d = (+4.7 +/- 1.3)e-4,  chi^2_nu = 1.44

CONCLUSION: the shifts do NOT grow with tan(theta); a constant shift
explains them as well as both terms together. No expansion is detected.
Upper limit (heating can only increase d, so one-sided, 2 sigma):
delta_d/d < 4.5e-4, i.e. the crystal warmed by less than ~11 K.

    WHY not use the per-line "delta_d_over_d" column of the peak table: it
    assumes each shift is pure expansion. At n=1 a small constant angle
    shift then looks like a big expansion (cot theta is large), giving
    ~9e-4, an overestimate. Fitting all lines together separates the two.
    WHY chi^2_nu so far below 1 (0.09): the centre errors from 4.3 were
    already scaled up by sqrt(chi^2_nu) of each peak fit, so they are
    generous. That is fine for an upper limit (conservative).

TWO NUMBERS FOR THE ERROR BUDGET:
  - THERMAL: the lab temperature during the real measurements is uncertain
    by sigma_T, so d is uncertain by

        sigma_d/d = alpha sigma_T = 40e-6/K x 2 K = 8e-5

    sigma_T = 2 K is an ASSUMPTION (set at the top of the file). Replace it
    with the recorded lab temperature if you have one. This error is
    applied to d itself, so it reaches every wavelength (5.10).
  - ANGLE STABILITY: delta_beta_0 = 0.0147 deg is an angle drift between
    two runs taken the same day. That is the SAME kind of error as the
    repeatability of 4.4 (0.0143 deg), and the same size. Adding both
    would count one effect twice, so fits_copy.py uses the repeatability
    and prints this only as an independent cross-check. The agreement of
    the two (0.0143 vs 0.0147) is good evidence that ~0.014 deg is the real
    run-to-run angle reproducibility.

HANDS TO fits_copy.py:  thermal_d_over_d = 8e-5,
                        sigma_stability = 0.0147 deg (cross-check only).

5.3  correct(scan, gate_time, tau, offset)
------------------------------------------
Every scan goes through this first. It returns four arrays:

    measured angle   kept only for SELECTING points (see below)
    true angle       measured angle - offset
    true rate        N = R / (1 - R tau)
    error on N       sigma_R / (1 - R tau)^2,  sigma_R = sqrt(max(R t, 1))/t

Size of the dead-time correction at each scan's highest rate:
    full x1.21, n=1 x1.21, n=2 x1.22, n=3 x1.04

CHOICES AND WHY
  - Errors from the RAW counts, before any correction or subtraction
    (3.1), then carried through the dead-time formula (3.2).
  - SELECT POINTS BY MEASURED ANGLE: every peak window and flank window in
    the code was read off a plot of the measured angle. The data sit on a
    0.1 deg grid. If the windows were applied to the shifted angles, a
    0.062 deg shift (or the +/-0.064 deg alignment variants) could push an
    edge point in or out. With only 9 points in the n=1 peak, losing one
    changes the fit noticeably, and the variants would then measure the
    effect of losing a point rather than the effect of the angle shift.
    Selecting on the measured angle keeps exactly the same points in every
    variant; only their x-values move.

5.4  The error variants
-----------------------
The method of 3.6, with these sources:

    source         moved                     by +/-       from
    dead time      tau                       5.0 us       deadtime.py
    zero offset    every angle               0.0060 deg   angle_offset.py
    alignment      every angle               0.0637 deg   alignment_error.py
    repeatability  every angle               0.0143 deg   alignment_error.py

That is 1 nominal + 4 x 2 = 9 complete runs of the analysis (dict
"variants", built from dict "error_sources"). Every fit below is done for
every variant. report() prints each result as

    value +/- total   (fit ..., dead time ..., zero offset ..., alignment ...,
                       repeatability ..., [extra])

and returns the totals, which are the ones written on the figures. The
figures themselves always show the nominal variant.

Two errors are not variants and are passed to report() as "extra":
    fit range   bremsstrahlung only (5.6)
    thermal     wavelengths only (5.10)

CHOICES AND WHY
  - Alignment and repeatability are errors in WHERE THE ANGLE SCALE REALLY
    IS, exactly like the zero offset. So they are applied the same way: by
    moving every angle. This reuses the existing machinery, and it
    automatically gives the right answer everywhere, including exactly
    zero for differences of angles (3.7).
  - Repeatability is added in quadrature with the fit error. Strictly, the
    repeatability scatter already contains some fit noise (from the noisy
    1 s scans it was measured on). But the close-up scans used here have
    fit errors of only 0.001-0.008 deg, so the double counting is tiny
    (e.g. sqrt(0.0143^2 + 0.004^2) = 0.0148). Slightly conservative,
    much simpler.
  - Applied as ONE shift to all scans at once, although each scan was a
    separate run. For any single result (one centre, U, one separation)
    this makes no difference. It would matter only for a result combining
    two separate scans, and none is quoted.

5.6  Checkmark 1: bremsstrahlung fit and tube voltage
-----------------------------------------------------
STEP A - REMOVE THE PEAKS. peak_windows lists each characteristic line
(measured angle, deg):

    14.2-14.9  K-beta n=1         29.6-30.1  K-beta n=2
    15.2-15.6  W L-alpha1 n=1     33.0-33.7  K-alpha n=2
    15.8-16.4  K-alpha n=1        47.7-48.0  K-beta n=3
                                  54.8-55.5  K-alpha n=3

Points inside any window are dropped; 553 of 599 remain.

STEP B - ANGLE TO WAVELENGTH with Bragg's law, n=1.

STEP C - THE MODEL (2.3):

    I(lambda) = A (lambda/lambda_min - 1)/lambda^2 exp(-B (lambda/100 pm)^3)
                for lambda > lambda_min, 0 below
    free: A (scale), lambda_min, B

STEP D - THE FIT (fit_background, through weighted_fit):
    dead-time-corrected rates, Poisson errors for 1 s, true angles.
    chi^2_nu = 1.84 (550 dof), so fit errors x1.36. Run for every variant.

STEP E - FIT-RANGE CHECK. The same fit to the background below 10 deg only
(76 points): lambda_min = 35.19 pm (U = 35.23 kV) against 34.60 pm (U =
35.83 kV) for the full range. The WHOLE difference is a "fit range"
systematic.

STEP F - DUANE-HUNT. U = 1239.84 / lambda_min, fit error propagated (3.2),
the other errors from the variants and the fit-range check.

FIGURE 2: background points with error bars and the Kramers fit.

CHOICES AND WHY
  - FIT THE WHOLE SPECTRUM SHAPE, not just the edge. lambda_min is where
    the curve reaches zero, but right at the edge the counts are tiny
    (0-20/s) and the edge is smeared by the instrument resolution. A model
    of the whole hump uses all 553 points to pin down where it must start.
  - Alternative tried: a straight line through the rising edge,
    extrapolated to zero rate (the "textbook" Duane-Hunt method). It gave
    U = 36.3 to 37.9 kV depending on which few points were used: far too
    sensitive to the choice of points with 0.1 deg steps. Not used.
  - WHY THE FIT-RANGE CHECK: the full-range fit also has to describe the
    long tail, where the simple model is weakest (empirical absorption
    term, 2nd-order bremsstrahlung above 7.05 deg, tungsten lines left in
    the background). A model that is wrong in the tail can drag
    lambda_min. Figure 2 shows the model undershooting the data around
    5-10 deg, and chi^2_nu = 1.84 says it is not perfect. Fitting only near
    the onset is the obvious test of how much that matters.
  - WHY 10 deg: below ~7 deg there is no 2nd-order bremsstrahlung; 10 deg
    keeps enough points past the top of the hump (~6 deg) to fix its
    shape. The near-edge fit has chi^2_nu = 1.06: it describes its region
    well.
  - WHY THE WHOLE DIFFERENCE, not half: the variants move an input by a
    known +/-1 sigma, so half the swing is right. Here there are just two
    reasonable analyses and no way to say which is correct; the honest
    error is the full difference between them.
  - ABSOLUTE ERRORS ON THE PEAKS ARE NOT FOR U: angle errors matter most
    here. The onset is at ~3.6 deg; a 0.064 deg shift there is 1.8% of the
    angle, so 1.8% of lambda_min and of U (0.63 kV). The same angle error
    at 55 deg is only 0.12% of the wavelength (2.7).

5.7  Checkmark 2: isolating each K-alpha peak - subtract_background()
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Run for every variant, each with its own Kramers fit.

  1. CROP the scan to [left flank start, right flank end]. This removes
     neighbouring features (e.g. W L-alpha1 at ~15.4 deg next to n=1).
  2. SUBTRACT the Kramers fit from 5.6 (only matters at n=1; by 33 and 55
     deg the bremsstrahlung is ~0).
  3. FIT A STRAIGHT LINE to the FLANKS only (the flat parts either side of
     the peak) and subtract it. This removes any leftover offset or tilt.
  4. Return the cropped true angles, the baseline-free rates, the raw-count
     errors, and the slope.

    flanks (measured deg)            points after cropping   flank points
    n=1: 15.7-15.8 and 16.4-16.5     9                       4
    n=2: 32.3-32.9 and 33.8-35.5     33                      25
    n=3: 53.5-54.5 and 55.7-56.5     31                      20

    tilt removed (1/s per deg): n=1 -1.79, n=2 -2.35, n=3 0.00

CHOICES AND WHY
  - A straight line, not a curve: over 1-3 deg the remaining background is
    nearly flat; a line is the simplest shape that can remove a tilt. A
    higher polynomial could start bending into the peak.
  - Fitted to the FLANKS ONLY, so the line cannot "eat" part of the peak.
  - Errors stay the raw-count errors (3.1): subtracting a smooth curve

  - n=1 has only 4 flank points (the close-up scan is only 1 deg wide).
    That is the weakest baseline of the three; a wider n=1 scan would help.

MAKIMA SPLINE (display only, 3.8):
  - a plain cubic spline overshoots and "rings" where a sharp peak meets a
    flat base;
  - PCHIP cannot put a maximum anywhere except at a data point;
  - makima (modified Akima) avoids most overshoot and lets the maximum fall
    between points.

5.8  Single-Gaussian fit (Figure 4)
-----------------------------------
gaussian(x, amplitude, mean, sigma), fitted through weighted_fit with the
raw-count errors. Start: height = highest point, centre = its angle, width
0.1 deg. chi^2_nu printed: 14.8, 2.8, 10.4 for n = 1, 2, 3.

What it means: the peak is really the K-alpha1 + K-alpha2 doublet, so one
Gaussian finds the intensity-weighted CENTRE of the pair. Compare it with
the weighted mean wavelength (2 x 154.06 + 154.44)/3 = 154.19 pm, not with
K-alpha1. Its width is inflated by the splitting, most at n=3 (sigma 0.140
deg against 0.097 in the two-line fit). The large chi^2_nu at n=1 and n=3
says one Gaussian is not the right shape - which is the point: it motivates
the two-Gaussian fits.

All angle errors (zero offset, alignment, repeatability) go straight onto
every fitted centre; alignment (0.064 deg) is 10-60x the fit error. Dead
time changes heights, not centres.

5.9  Two-Gaussian fits (Figures 5 and 6)
----------------------------------------
FREE FIT (Figure 5), two_gaussians: all six parameters free.
  - Starting guesses from physics: splitting from 2.5; K-alpha1 at
    centre - split/3 and K-alpha2 at centre + 2 split/3 (with a 2:1
    intensity ratio the centroid is one third of the way from K-alpha1 to
    K-alpha2); heights 2:1.
  - Bounds: heights and widths >= 0, centres inside the scan.
  - It is a COMPARISON fit. With nothing tying the two Gaussians to the
    physics it gives unphysical answers: at n=1 K-alpha2 comes out taller
    than K-alpha1 with huge errors; at n=2 the second Gaussian shrinks to
    A2 ~ 7/s off to one side. Do not quote it. Its failure is the reason
    for the constrained fit.

CONSTRAINED FIT (Figure 6), two_gaussians_fixed_ratio: free A1, mu1, mu2
and one shared sigma (4 parameters, not 6):

  (1) SHARED WIDTH: both lines are broadened by the same instrument at
      almost the same angle, so they must have the same width.
  (2) FIXED INTENSITY RATIO, A2 = 0.518 A1. Intensity is AREA (3.9).
      Because the widths are equal, fixing the height ratio really fixes
      the area ratio. (If each line had its own width, the fit could
      change K-alpha2's intensity by making it wider or narrower, and the
      "fixed" ratio would not be fixed.)

    WHY constrain rather than leave free: two Gaussians 0.04-0.2 deg apart
    inside a 0.1-0.14 deg-wide peak are nearly impossible to separate
    freely: the fit can trade height for width for position. Physics
    removes that freedom.
    WHY mu2 is still free (not fixed at mu1 + predicted split): leaving it
    free lets the data MEASURE the splitting, which is then a genuine test
    of 2.5 (5.10).
    CHECK on the fixed ratio: changing 0.518 to 0.50 or 0.53 moves mu1 by
    at most 0.0015 deg and the n=3 separation by at most 0.0004 deg. That
    is negligible against the other errors, so no error is added for it.

chi^2_nu printed: 8.6, 2.8, 4.9. Still above 1 (the peak shape is not
exactly two Gaussians, and n=1 has only 9 points), so errors are scaled up
(3.4). Results are expanded back to six numbers [A1, mu1, sigma, 0.518 A1,
mu2, sigma] so Figure 6 is drawn the same way as Figure 5.

5.11 chi_squared.py - which peak model describes the data better?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Compares the single Gaussian (5.8) with the constrained two-Gaussian fit,
I2/I1 = 0.518 and shared width (5.9), for each order. It imports
fits_copy.py, so it uses exactly the same peaks, fits and splines (nominal
variant); the figures are drawn off-screen and the printout of
fits_copy.py is silenced.

For each order and model it prints:

    N, k, dof    points, free parameters, dof = N - k - 2
    chi2         sum of ((data - model)/error)^2, raw-count Poisson errors
    chi2_nu      chi2/dof, +/- sqrt(2/dof)
    p-value      chance of a chi2 this large from counting noise alone
    vs spline    RMS gap between fitted curve and spline, % of peak height

and then, for each order, which model is better and whether the second
line is really needed.

CHOICES AND WHY
  - dof = N - k - 2, not N - k: the straight baseline (slope, intercept)
    was fitted to the flanks of these same points (5.7), so it used up 2
    degrees of freedom. The Kramers background came from the full scan, a
    different data set, so it costs nothing here. (fits_copy.py's
    weighted_fit uses N - k for its error scaling, so its printed chi2_nu
    are slightly lower: e.g. 14.8 against 22.2 for n=1 with 9 points.)
  - THE ERROR ON chi2_nu is sqrt(2/dof): if the model were right and the
    only scatter were counting noise, chi2_nu would scatter about 1 by
    this much. So a chi2_nu more than ~2 of these errors from 1 means the
    model misses something real. Few points (n=1: dof 3-4) means a large
    error, i.e. a weak test.
  - CHI-SQUARED AGAINST THE DATA, NOT THE SPLINE. The spline passes
    exactly through every data point, so at the measured angles a chi2
    against the spline IS the chi2 against the data. Between the points
    the spline only interpolates. Evaluating chi2 on 2000 spline points
    would invent 2000 "measurements" with no errors and fake the dof
    (3.8). So the spline enters only as the descriptive "vs spline"
    column, with no statistical claim.
  - THE F-TEST. Setting mu2 = mu1 in the two-Gaussian model gives a single
    Gaussian (of height 1.518 A1), so the single Gaussian is a special
    case of it: the models are NESTED. Adding a parameter always lowers
    chi2 a little; the F-test asks whether it lowered it by more than
    chance:

        F = (chi2_single - chi2_double) / (chi2_double / dof_double)

    with 1 and dof_double degrees of freedom. Dividing by the two-Gaussian
    chi2_nu (rather than assuming it is 1) keeps the test fair when
    chi2_nu > 1. p < 0.05: the second line is needed.

RESULTS
    order  model               dof  chi2_nu        p-value   vs spline
    n=1    single Gaussian       4  22.2 +/- 0.7   2e-18     3.1%
           2 Gaussians, 0.518    3  14.4 +/- 0.8   2e-09     3.0%
    n=2    single Gaussian      28   3.0 +/- 0.3   1e-07     0.8%
           2 Gaussians, 0.518   27   3.0 +/- 0.3   2e-07     0.8%
    n=3    single Gaussian      26  11.2 +/- 0.3   2e-46     3.9%
           2 Gaussians, 0.518   25   5.2 +/- 0.3   2e-16     2.0%

    F-test for the second line:
      n=1  F = 3.2,  p = 0.17     not needed
      n=2  F = 1.0,  p = 0.32     not needed
      n=3  F = 30.4, p = 1e-05    NEEDED

WHAT IT MEANS
  - n=3: the two-Gaussian model is clearly better (chi2_nu halves, the
    curve sits twice as close to the spline, F-test p = 1e-5). This is
    exactly where 2.5 says the doublet is resolved (0.20 deg splitting).
  - n=1 and n=2: the second line is not justified by the data, as 2.5
    predicts (0.04 and 0.09 deg splitting, inside the peak width). At n=2
    both models are equally good; at n=1, with only 9 points, the drop in
    chi2 is not significant.
  - Neither model reaches chi2_nu ~ 1 in any order. Why is explained
    below. It does not affect the comparison: both models share the same
    shape assumption, and the F-test allows for it.
  - So: quote the constrained two-Gaussian fit for n=3 (mu1, the
    separation, the wavelength). For n=1 and n=2 the single Gaussian
    describes the data as well, and its centre (the doublet centroid) is
    the safer number.

WHY ARE THE REDUCED CHI-SQUARED VALUES SO HIGH?
Short answer: the data are precise enough to show that the real peak shape
is not exactly a Gaussian. The error bars are right; the model shape is
not perfect. Each possible cause was tested, as follows.

  (1) ARE THE POISSON ERROR BARS TOO SMALL? No.
      - The flat baseline points either side of each peak (no peak, only
        counting noise) give chi2 ~ 1.3-1.5 per point (30 for 23 points at
        n=2, 31-32 for 21 points at n=3). Badly underestimated error bars
        would show up here too.
      - Two INDEPENDENT scans of the n=2 peak, both Friday, 10 s per step:
        2nd_Order_Good (1.0 mA) and 2nd_Order_High (0.6 mA). After
        dead-time correction, one should be the other times a constant.
        Fitting a scale factor (1.70; current ratio 1.67) and an angle
        shift between the runs, they agree point by point with
            chi2_nu = 1.13
        So the counting errors are correct AND the peak shape is the same
        from run to run.
      - The shift needed was 0.0136 +/- 0.0011 deg: the run-to-run
        repeatability of 4.4 (0.0143 deg), confirmed by a completely
        separate method.

  (2) THE DATA ARE VERY PRECISE. At 10 s per step the n=2 peak top is
      ~13,000 counts, so each point is known to < 1%. The fitted curves
      miss the measured shape by 1-4% of the peak height (the "vs spline"
      column). A 3% miss with 1% error bars is 3 sigma per point, and
      chi2_nu ~ 10 follows directly. With 1 s per step (error bars
      sqrt(10) larger) the same misfit would give a chi2_nu 10x smaller.

  (3) WHERE THE MISFIT IS (largest (data - model)/error, constrained fit):

      n=2  50 of the 85 chi2 comes from 6 TAIL points just outside the
           peak (+4.5, +3.8 at 33.64 and 33.74 deg; +2.8 at 32.94 deg),
           where the data sit ABOVE the Gaussian. The real peak has
           heavier tails than a Gaussian, mostly on the high-angle side.
           The core fits well.
      n=3  even with two lines, the 5 core points alternate above and
           below the model (+4.5, -4.8, +3.9, -3.0). Each line's shape is
           not a perfect Gaussian, and 5 points across a doublet at a
           0.1 deg step is coarse sampling. There are also tail points
           above the model (+4.6 at 54.74 deg).
      n=1  only 9 points, 3 in the peak. The 0.1 deg step is about the
           same as the peak width (sigma 0.07-0.08 deg), so the peak is
           badly under-sampled, and the unresolved doublet distorts its
           shape. dof is only 3-4, so chi2_nu is itself uncertain
           (+/-0.7-0.8), but 14-22 is still far from 1.

  (4) RULED OUT:
      - ANGLE JITTER from step to step. Adding an angle error sigma_theta
        x (slope of the peak) to each error bar would need 35-600 mdeg to
        reach chi2_nu = 1 (repeatability is only 14 mdeg), and it cannot
        explain misfits on the flat tails, where the slope is ~0.
      - TUBE INTENSITY FLICKER. Adding a fraction f of the rate to each
        error bar would need f = 30-1400%.
      - DEAD TIME. n=2 has the same peak rate as n=1 (~1000/s raw) and
        its core fits well.
      - THE BASELINE. Its points scatter normally (point (1)).

  (5) A BETTER SHAPE HELPS A LITTLE. A pseudo-Voigt (a mix of Gaussian
      and Lorentzian, i.e. heavier tails) was tried as a test, not in
      the code: chi2_nu falls from 3.0 to 2.6 at n=2 and from 5.2 to 4.5
      at n=3. The centres change by < 0.003 deg and the n=3 separation by
      0.001 deg, far inside the quoted errors. The remaining misfit (tail
      asymmetry, sampling) would need a measured instrument profile.

WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT
  - Nothing more in the code. The standard response, scaling the fit
    errors up by sqrt(chi2_nu) (3.4), is already applied.
  - Do NOT inflate the error bars to force chi2_nu down to 1. Point (1)
    shows they are already right; inflating them would hide a real
    feature of the data.
  - In the write-up: "chi2_nu >> 1 because the instrumental line profile
    is not exactly Gaussian; baseline points and two independent scans
    (chi2_nu = 1.13) confirm that the Poisson errors are correct."
  - With more beam time: a finer step (0.02-0.05 deg) over the n=1 peak.

A WARNING about other Friday n=2 scans: 2nd_Order, 2nd_Order_Long (both
0.77 mA) and 2nd_Order_Long_2nd (0.9 mA) do NOT agree with the rest. Two
scans at the same current differ in intensity by ~5x. They were probably
taken while the setup was still being adjusted. They are not used anywhere
in this analysis and should not be.

5.12 Voigt line shape (Figure 7)
--------------------------------
The last block of fits_copy.py fits each peak with the standard line shape
of X-ray diffraction, the VOIGT profile: a Gaussian (width sigma, the
instrument: slits, beam divergence, crystal mosaic) blurred by a
Lorentzian (half width gamma, the natural line shape and crystal effects).
gamma = 0 gives back a plain Gaussian, so the Voigt can only fit as well
as the Gaussian or better.

    voigt(x, amplitude, mean, sigma, gamma)
        amplitude = peak HEIGHT (scipy's voigt_profile is divided by its
        value at the centre)
    two_voigts_fixed_ratio(x, A1, mean_1, mean_2, sigma, gamma)
        the same constraints as the two-Gaussian fit (5.9): I2/I1 = 0.518
        and both lines share sigma AND gamma, so the height ratio is still
        the area ratio

Starting guesses come from the Gaussian fits, with gamma = 0.01 deg. Fits
go through weighted_fit (3.4). NOMINAL VARIANT ONLY: this is a check of the
line shape, not a new result, so the quoted centres and wavelengths stay
those of the Gaussian fits (5.8-5.10).

FIGURE 7: data, one Voigt, the two Voigts and their sum, for each order,
with the expected Bragg angles and arrows as on Figure 6 (5.9). Its legend
is one size smaller (7 pt) so it clears the chi2 box.
The box on each panel gives chi2 = chi2/dof = chi2_nu +/- sqrt(2/dof) for
both Voigt models, and for the two Gaussian models for comparison. dof =
N - k - 2, as in chi_squared.py (5.11). The same numbers are printed.

RESULTS (chi2_nu +/- error)

    order  one Gaussian   one Voigt      two Gaussians  two Voigts
    n=1    22.2 +/- 0.7   29.6 +/- 0.8   14.4 +/- 0.8   21.6 +/- 1.0
    n=2     3.0 +/- 0.3    2.6 +/- 0.3    3.0 +/- 0.3    2.5 +/- 0.3
    n=3    11.2 +/- 0.3   11.5 +/- 0.3    5.2 +/- 0.3    4.3 +/- 0.3

    fitted Lorentzian half width gamma (two Voigts): 0.000, 0.003, 0.012 deg
    against Gaussian sigma 0.069, 0.087, 0.087 deg

WHAT IT MEANS
  - n=1: gamma goes to 0, so the Voigt IS the Gaussian: same chi2 (88.7,
    43.2), but one fewer dof, so a higher chi2_nu. With 9 points nothing
    better than a Gaussian can be seen.
  - n=2: a little Lorentzian (gamma ~0.003 deg) gives the heavier tails
    seen in 5.11 and lowers chi2_nu from 3.0 to 2.5-2.6. One Voigt already
    does as well as two: the doublet is not resolved here, as 2.5 says.
  - n=3: two Voigts beat two Gaussians (5.2 -> 4.3), and both beat a
    single line by far (the doublet is resolved). What is left is mainly
    the ASYMMETRY of the n=3 peak (wider on the low-angle side), which a
    symmetric Voigt cannot follow; an asymmetric ("split") profile brings
    chi2_nu to ~1.8 in a test fit.
  - The Lorentzian part is small (gamma is 0-14% of sigma): the peaks are
    almost Gaussian, as expected when the instrument sets the width (2.6).
    But it GROWS with order, for a physical reason (next part).

WHY THE VOIGT HELPS MORE AT HIGHER ORDERS: THE NATURAL LINE WIDTH
Each peak's width has two parts that behave differently with angle:

  - INSTRUMENT (the Gaussian part, sigma): slits, beam divergence, crystal
    mosaic. Roughly the same at every angle.
  - NATURAL LINE WIDTH (the Lorentzian part, gamma): Cu K-alpha1 itself is
    ~2.3 eV wide out of 8048 eV, a fixed fraction of its wavelength,
    delta_lambda/lambda = 2.9e-4. Through Bragg's law (2.7) this becomes
    an ANGLE width of

        FWHM (rad) = tan(theta) * delta_lambda / lambda,   gamma = FWHM/2

    so it grows with tan(theta): the crystal spreads the wavelengths out
    more at high orders.

    order  tan(theta)  predicted gamma   fitted gamma (two Voigts)
    n=1    0.29        0.0023 deg        0.0000 +/- 0.0028 deg
    n=2    0.66        0.0054 deg        0.0029 +/- 0.0011 deg
    n=3    1.43        0.0117 deg        0.0118 +/- 0.0045 deg

  - n=1: far below what 9 points can detect; the fit finds 0, consistent
    with the prediction.
  - n=2: detected, about half the prediction (2.3 sigma low).
  - n=3: agrees with the prediction.
  - With the Lorentzian part separated out, the instrument's Gaussian width
    is the SAME at n=2 and n=3 (sigma = 0.087 deg both). The two-Gaussian
    fit had to absorb the natural width into a wider sigma at n=3 (0.097
    deg). That is a good consistency check: the instrument does not
    change, the natural width scales with tan(theta).

  So the Voigt improves the fit more at high orders because that is where
  the Lorentzian part becomes large enough to matter, not because anything
  about the doublet changes.

  CAVEATS: at n=3 the detection is only 2.6 sigma; part of gamma may be
  absorbing the peak's asymmetry; and 2.3 eV is an approximate natural
  width. Quote this as "consistent with the natural line width", not as
  a measurement of it.

  Note also that chi2_nu does NOT simply improve with order (two Voigts:
  21.6, 2.5, 4.3). Values from different scans are not directly
  comparable: each has its own number of points per peak, count level
  (so error-bar size) and, at n=3, an asymmetric peak. What grows with
  order is how much the Voigt improves on the Gaussian.

CHECK: THE SEPARATION AS A WAVELENGTH
A better chi2 does not push the separation either way: mean_2 is a free
parameter. In ANGLE the separation must grow with order,
n delta_lambda / (2 d cos theta) (2.5); in WAVELENGTH it is fixed at
154.44 - 154.06 = 0.38 pm. Converting each fitted separation back,

    delta_lambda = 2 d cos(mean_1) * (mean_2 - mean_1) / n    (angle in rad)

    order  sigma / separation   two Gaussians   two Voigts   reference
    n=1    1.7  (hidden)        0.93 pm         0.93 pm      0.38 pm
    n=2    1.0  (partly)        0.28 pm         0.31 pm      0.38 pm
    n=3    0.5  (resolved)      0.387 pm        0.383 pm     0.38 pm

  - n=3 measures the K-alpha splitting to ~1%.
  - n=1 is meaningless: two lines 1.7 widths apart inside one peak cannot
    be located separately, so the fit's separation is not a measurement
    (its small fit error hides how strongly it trades off against the
    width).
  - The Voigt fit moves the n=2 AND n=3 separations towards the reference
    (0.28 -> 0.31 pm, 0.387 -> 0.383 pm). It does not push the lines
    closer together.
  - Rule of thumb: a doublet separation can be measured once it is about
    as large as the peak width (sigma / separation <~ 1).

FOR THE WRITE-UP: "The doublet is resolved only at n=3, which gives
delta_lambda = 0.383 pm (reference 0.38 pm). The Lorentzian width of the
Voigt fits grows with order as expected for the natural line width
(proportional to tan theta), while the instrumental Gaussian width stays
constant at ~0.087 deg."
  - The RESULTS barely move: mean 1 is 16.0131 / 33.2731 / 55.0711 deg
    with two Voigts against 16.0131 / 33.2759 / 55.0703 deg with two
    Gaussians, differences of at most 0.003 deg against a total error of
    0.066 deg. The Gaussian results can be quoted with confidence; the
    Voigt fit shows the line shape is not what limits them.

5.13 Pseudo-Voigt line shape (Figure 8)
---------------------------------------
A copy of 5.12 with the PSEUDO-VOIGT, the approximation to the Voigt used
by most diffraction software: a weighted SUM (not a convolution) of a
Gaussian and a Lorentzian of the same FWHM,

    pseudo_voigt(x, amplitude, mean, fwhm, eta)
        = amplitude * [eta * L + (1 - eta) * G],  both of unit height
        eta = 0 pure Gaussian, eta = 1 pure Lorentzian
    two_pseudo_voigts_fixed_ratio(x, A1, mean_1, mean_2, fwhm, eta)
        same constraints as 5.12: I2/I1 = 0.518, shared fwhm AND eta

It has one width, so it cannot split the broadening into instrument
(sigma) and natural line (gamma) as the Voigt does. voigt_fwhm_eta(sigma,
gamma) converts a Voigt into its FWHM and the eta of the best-matching
pseudo-Voigt (Thompson, Cox and Hastings 1987); the Voigt fits of 5.12
give the starting guesses and the comparison. NOMINAL VARIANT ONLY, as in
5.12.

FIGURE 8: as Figure 7 with pseudo-Voigts; the box adds the fitted eta and
the Voigt chi2 for comparison. The printout gives each pseudo-Voigt
result with the Voigt equivalent in brackets, and the change from Voigt
to pseudo-Voigt in units of the pseudo-Voigt fit error.

RESULTS (two lines, I2/I1 = 0.518)

    order  chi2_nu: Voigt  pseudo-V   eta fitted     eta from Voigt
    n=1    21.6 +/- 1.0    21.6       0.000 +/- 0.031  0.000
    n=2     2.50 +/- 0.28   2.53      0.024 +/- 0.010  0.038
    n=3     4.27 +/- 0.29   4.47      0.088 +/- 0.038  0.142

    order  mean 1 shift   separation (deg)          delta_lambda (pm)
           pV - Voigt     pseudo-V         Voigt    pseudo-V        Voigt
    n=1    +0.0000        0.0986 +/- 0.0120 0.0986  0.933            0.933
    n=2    +0.0004        0.0747 +/- 0.0162 0.0759  0.307 +/- 0.067  0.312
    n=3    -0.0001        0.2045 +/- 0.0067 0.2039  0.384 +/- 0.013  0.383

WHAT IT MEANS
  - NO MEASUREMENT CHANGES. Every centre moves by <= 0.0004 deg (<= 0.07
    fit sigma), every separation by <= 0.08 fit sigma, and the K-alpha1
    wavelengths are identical to 0.01 pm. The line-shape model is not a
    systematic error on any quoted result.
  - n=1: both shapes collapse to a pure Gaussian (gamma = 0, eta = 0), so
    the fits are the same fit. The n=1 separation is wrong (0.93 pm
    against 0.38 pm) for both shapes alike: it is not a line-shape
    problem but the unresolved doublet (5.12).
  - n=2, n=3: the true Voigt fits slightly better (n=3: chi2 102.5 against
    107.3 for the same dof), and the fitted eta comes out ~35% below the
    eta implied by the Voigt. With one shared width the pseudo-Voigt
    cannot reproduce the Voigt's tails exactly; the extra freedom of
    separate sigma and gamma is worth a little chi2 at n=3.
  - eta GROWS with order (0, 0.024, 0.088), the same trend as gamma in
    5.12: the Lorentzian share rises as the natural width spreads out with
    tan(theta). The pseudo-Voigt sees the trend but cannot say whether it
    comes from the instrument or the line; the Voigt can, which is why the
    Voigt stays the line-shape check of record.

================================================================================
PART 9 - LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
================================================================================

1. LEFTOVER ANGLE OFFSET. After removing the 0.062 deg zero offset, the
   K-alpha1 peaks still sit above Bragg's law by +0.16, +0.16, +0.04 deg
   (n = 1, 2, 3). With the alignment error included these are 2.5, 2.5 and
   0.6 sigma: no longer a gross disagreement, but n=1 and n=2 need
   explaining. It is not a constant, so a single zero offset cannot be the
   whole story. Candidates:
     - the CRYSTAL's own zero, which the direct-beam scan cannot see (4.2);
     - whether the direct-beam angle is theta or 2 theta (4.2);
     - a small error in the tube voltage display, which would trade off
       against the angle scale in Checkmark 1 (4.4).
   Next step: use the known Cu lines themselves as the calibration. Fit
   the measured centres of K-alpha AND K-beta in all orders with
   theta_measured = arcsin(n lambda / 2d) + theta_0 (+ maybe a scale
   term). That finds the crystal's zero from the data.

2. TUNGSTEN LINES IN THE BACKGROUND. W L-alpha1 at n=1 is masked, but other
   W lines and the 2nd/3rd-order images of W L-alpha (~31.8 and ~51.8 deg)
   are still inside the Kramers fit and add to chi^2_nu = 1.84. Masking
   them is easy: add their windows to peak_windows.

3. HIGHER-ORDER BREMSSTRAHLUNG above 7.05 deg is not in the model. The
   fit-range check (5.6) covers its effect on lambda_min empirically. A
   model with an n=2 term would be the proper fix.

4. SHARP CUT-OFF. The model switches on abruptly at lambda_min, but the
   instrument resolution (sigma ~0.085 deg, ~2.4% of the angle at 3.6 deg)
   smears the real edge. Convolving the model with a Gaussian of the
   measured width would fix it. Partly covered by the fit-range check.

5. FIGURE 1 OVERLAY uses an offset; dividing by the current would be
   right (5.5). Display only.

6. n=1 DOUBLET UNRESOLVED (0.040 deg splitting, below the width). mu2 - mu1
   at n=1 is meaningless. Option: fix mu2 = mu1 + predicted split at n=1.

7. ASSUMED NUMBERS: sigma_T = 2 K (4.5). Use the recorded lab
   temperature if there is one.

8. THE n=1 CLOSE-UP SCAN is only 1 deg wide (4 flank points). A wider scan
   would give a much better baseline.

9. ALIGNMENT AT HIGH ANGLE is extrapolated: the flipped scans stop at
   30 deg, so the 0.0637 deg is assumed to hold at 33 and 55 deg (justified
   by b = 0 in 4.4, but not measured).

PART 10 - GLOSSARY

  bremsstrahlung    smooth X-ray continuum from electrons slowing down
  centroid          intensity-weighted mean position, model-free
  chi^2_nu          chi-squared per degree of freedom; ~1 means good fit
  correlated errors errors shared between results; don't average down
  coupled mode      detector arm turns at twice the crystal angle
  dead time (tau)   time a GM tube is blind after each count
  dof               degrees of freedom = points - fitted parameters
  doublet           two lines very close together (K-alpha1, K-alpha2)
  Duane-Hunt law    lambda_min = hc/(eU), shortest bremsstrahlung wavelength
  FWHM              full width at half maximum = 2.355 sigma for a Gaussian
  gate time         counting time per angle step (1 s or 10 s here)
  goniometer        the turntable carrying crystal and detector
  Kramers' law      theoretical shape of the bremsstrahlung spectrum
  makima            modified Akima spline; smooth, no overshoot
  mosaic spread     small misorientations of blocks inside a real crystal
  order (n)         which multiple of lambda satisfies Bragg's law
  pcov              covariance matrix of fitted parameters
  Poisson noise     sqrt(N) scatter of a count N
  quadrature        sqrt(a^2 + b^2 + ...): how independent errors add
  systematic error  shared by every point; does not shrink with more data
  variant           a full rerun with one input moved by +/-1 sigma
  zero offset       angle reading at the true zero of the angle scale

  Main variables in fits_copy.py

    background_fits[variant]         Kramers fit (popt, perr, chi2_nu)
    peaks[variant]                   baseline-free (angle, rate, error, tilt)
    gaussian_fits, two_gaussian_fits,
    ratio_fits [variant]             fits per order
    error_sources, variants          the systematic-error machinery (5.4)
    report()                         prints value +/- total (breakdown)
    weighted_fit()                   curve_fit with the scaling rule (3.4)
