HOW TO READ THIS
----------------
Part 1 explains the physics and statistics the code relies on, from scratch.
Part 2 walks through the code in the order it runs, saying what each block
does and why. Part 3 lists the numbers it currently produces. Part 4 lists
what is not yet handled. If you already know the physics, skip to Part 2.


HOW TO RUN
----------
    pip install -r requirements.txt
    python fits_copy.py

The four CSV files must be in the same folder. Six figures open one at a
time; close each window to move on to the next. Fit results are printed to
the terminal as the script goes.

Every calibration and systematic error is worked out in its own file and
IMPORTED by fits_copy.py, so nothing has to be copied by hand. Redo a
calibration (new CSV) and fits_copy.py picks it up on its next run.

    file                  gives fits_copy.py                 from
    deadtime.py           tau, tau_err                       tube-current scan
    angle_offset.py       zero_offset, zero_offset_err       direct-beam scan
    alignment_error.py    sigma_align_constant,              flipped-crystal scans
                          sigma_repeat_pooled                repeat normal scans
    temperature_error.py  thermal_d_over_d                   heated/unheated scans
                          (sigma_stability, cross-check only)

Importing a file prints and plots nothing. Run one on its own
(python alignment_error.py) to see its full output and figure.
alignment_error.py and temperature_error.py read the peak tables in
"Error analysis CSVs", which build_error_csvs.py makes from the .xry files.


================================================================================
PART 1 - BASICS
================================================================================

1.1  Where the X-rays come from
-------------------------------
Electrons are accelerated through a voltage U (about 35 kV here) and hit a
copper anode. Two kinds of X-ray come out:

  (a) BREMSSTRAHLUNG ("braking radiation"). An electron is deflected and
      slowed by a nucleus and radiates a photon. It can lose any amount of
      energy, so this gives a smooth, CONTINUOUS spectrum: the broad hump
      that the peaks sit on.

  (b) CHARACTERISTIC LINES. An electron knocks a copper atom's innermost
      (K-shell, 1s) electron out. An outer electron drops into the hole and
      emits a photon of one exact energy, fixed by copper's energy levels:

          L -> K   gives K-alpha   (the strongest line)
          M -> K   gives K-beta    (weaker, shorter wavelength)

      K-alpha is really TWO lines very close together (a "doublet"):

          K-alpha1 : 2p(3/2) -> 1s   lambda = 154.06 pm
          K-alpha2 : 2p(1/2) -> 1s   lambda = 154.44 pm
          K-beta   :                 lambda = 139.22 pm

      The 2p(3/2) level has 4 quantum states and 2p(1/2) has 2, so
      K-alpha1 is about twice as intense: I(K-alpha2)/I(K-alpha1) ~ 0.5.
      (The code uses 0.518.)

1.2  The shortest wavelength: Duane-Hunt law
--------------------------------------------
An electron cannot give a photon more energy than it has, eU. The maximum
photon energy therefore sets a MINIMUM wavelength:

    lambda_min = h c / (e U)

With hc = 1239.84 keV pm this becomes

    U [kV] = 1239.84 / lambda_min [pm]

So measuring where the bremsstrahlung starts (lambda_min) measures the tube
voltage.

1.3  Turning angle into wavelength: Bragg's law
-----------------------------------------------
The NaCl crystal acts as a wavelength filter. X-rays reflecting off
successive planes of atoms (spacing d) interfere constructively only when

    n lambda = 2 d sin(theta)        n = 1, 2, 3, ...  ("order")

The goniometer turns the crystal to angle theta and the detector to 2 theta.
At each angle only wavelengths with n lambda = 2 d sin(theta) reach the
detector. Scanning theta therefore scans the spectrum.

  - NaCl has lattice constant a = 564.02 pm. The reflecting (200) planes
    have spacing d = a/2 = 282.01 pm (variable d_NaCl in the code).
  - The same K-alpha line appears at several angles, once per order:
    about 16 deg (n=1), 33 deg (n=2) and 55 deg (n=3).

How far apart the two K-alpha lines appear (differentiate Bragg's law):

    delta_theta = n * delta_lambda / (2 d cos(theta))     [radians]

With delta_lambda = 0.38 pm:

    n=1 : 0.040 deg   (not resolvable, smaller than the 0.1 deg step)
    n=2 : 0.093 deg   (partly resolved)
    n=3 : 0.204 deg   (clearly resolved, visible as a shoulder)

Higher orders spread the lines further apart. That is why the 3rd order is
the best place to see the doublet.

1.4  Counting statistics (Poisson noise)
----------------------------------------
The Geiger-Muller (GM) tube counts individual photons. Photon arrivals are
random, so a count N fluctuates by about

    sigma_N = sqrt(N)                (Poisson statistics)

The files store a RATE R = N / t, where t is the gate (counting) time per
angle step. Then sigma_R = sqrt(R / t).

KEY POINT: the noise belongs to the RAW number of counts the detector
recorded: peak photons PLUS background photons. Subtracting a smooth
background curve afterwards does not remove any noise. A point that measured
20/s still has the scatter of 20/s even if it reads ~0 after subtraction.

1.5  Least-squares fitting
--------------------------
A model f(x; p) has adjustable parameters p (for example the height,
centre and width of a peak). Fitting means choosing p to minimise

    chi^2 = sum over points i of  [ (y_i - f(x_i; p)) / sigma_i ]^2

  - sigma_i is the uncertainty on point i. A small sigma_i gives that point
    a large weight (1/sigma_i^2), so the fit tries harder to pass near it.
    The weights must therefore be right, or the best-fit values shift.
  - chi^2 / dof ("reduced chi-squared", chi^2_nu), with
    dof = (number of points) - (number of parameters), should be ~1 for a
    good model with correct uncertainties. Much greater than 1 means the
    model misses something, or the sigmas are too small.

scipy's curve_fit does this minimisation:

  - p0             starting guess for the parameters
  - bounds         allowed range for each parameter
  - sigma          per-point uncertainties (the weights)
  - absolute_sigma True:  trust sigma as real uncertainties.
                   False: only the RELATIVE sizes of sigma matter; the
                          overall scale is set from the scatter of the
                          data. Use this when the gate time is unknown.
  - returns popt (best-fit values) and pcov (covariance matrix; the square
    roots of its diagonal are the parameter uncertainties)

1.6  Interpolation is not fitting
---------------------------------
  - An INTERPOLATING SPLINE passes exactly through every data point,
    noise included. It is good for drawing a smooth line through the data.
  - A FIT uses a physical model with few parameters and goes through the
    data "on average", weighted by the uncertainties.

A spline evaluated at 2000 points contains no more information than the
data points it passes through. Fitting a model to the spline instead of the
data invents extra "measurements", ignores the uncertainties, and makes the
answer depend on the interpolation method. So in this code the spline is
for display only, and every fit is to the measured points.

1.7  Gaussians: height versus area
----------------------------------
    g(x) = A exp( -(x - mu)^2 / (2 sigma^2) )

    A     = peak HEIGHT (amplitude)
    mu    = centre (mean)
    sigma = width (FWHM = 2.355 sigma)
    AREA  = A * sigma * sqrt(2 pi)   <- this is the INTENSITY (photon count)

Two Gaussians with the same height but different widths have different
intensities. A ratio of intensities is therefore a ratio of AREAS. It equals
the ratio of heights only if the widths are the same.

The Gaussian shape is justified here because the measured peak width
(sigma ~ 0.1 deg) comes from the instrument (slit widths, beam divergence,
crystal mosaic). The lines' own natural width is only ~0.005 deg.

2.1  Loading the data
---------------------
  Cu_NaCl_long_1.2KPeak.csv  Full scan, 2.5-62.3 deg in 0.1 deg steps.
                             Integer rates (consistent with a 1 s gate).
                             Variables: measured_full (raw); angle, rate, sigma (corrected)
  1st_order_long.csv         15.5-16.5 deg, the n=1 K-alpha peak.
                             Variables: measured_n1
  2nd_Order_Good.csv         32.0-35.5 deg, the n=2 K-alpha peak.
                             Variables: measured_n2
  3rd_Order_Long_Good.csv    Column 1 repeats the n=2 scan; column 2 is the
                             n=3 scan (53.5-56.5 deg). Only rows with an
                             n=3 value are kept.
                             Variables: measured_n3

Settings stored in the .xry files (all at 35 kV):

  full scan   Thursday, 0.12 mA, 1 s per step
  n=1 scan    Thursday, 0.12 mA, 10 s per step
  n=2, n=3    Friday,   1.0 mA,  10 s per step   (Friday/Task1)

So the gate times are KNOWN, not assumed. The n=2 and n=3 scans were taken
at 8x the current, which is why they are ~7x more intense than the full
scan (2.3): 8x the photons, minus the extra dead-time loss at the higher
rate.

2.2  Corrections and error bars: correct(scan, gate_time, tau, offset)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Every scan goes through this before anything else. It returns four arrays:

  measured angle   kept only for SELECTING points (see below)
  true angle       theta_true = theta_measured - zero_offset
                   (zero_offset from the direct-beam scan, angle_offset.py)
  true rate        N = R / (1 - R tau)
                   (non-paralysable dead time, tau from deadtime.py)
  error on N       sigma_N = sigma_R / (1 - R tau)^2

sigma_R is the Poisson error of the RAW count, sqrt(R t) / t, with a floor
of one count so a zero-count point does not get sigma = 0. It is taken
BEFORE anything is subtracted, because the Poisson noise belongs to the raw
count (see 1.4). dN/dR = 1/(1 - R tau)^2 carries it through the dead-time
correction. These errors are the error bars on every figure and the
weights in every fit.

Gate time t: 1 s for the full scan and 10 s for the dedicated scans
(gate_full, gate_peaks), read from the .xry files (2.1).

Every fit goes through weighted_fit(), which trusts these Poisson error
bars (absolute_sigma=True). If chi^2/dof > 1 the model misses something
the error bars do not cover, so the parameter errors are scaled UP by
sqrt(chi^2/dof). They are never scaled DOWN when chi^2/dof < 1: the
counting noise is a hard floor. (Before, absolute_sigma=False scaled them
both ways, which shrank the errors of the n=1 two-Gaussian fit.)

Why select points by measured angle: every peak and flank window in the
code was read off a plot of the measured angle. The data lie on a 0.1 deg
grid, so shifting them by 0.062 deg against the same window numbers would
drop or add an edge point (the n=1 peak has only 9 points after cropping).
Selecting on the measured angle keeps exactly the same points as before.

Size of the dead-time correction at the highest rate in each scan:
  full scan x1.21, n=1 x1.21, n=2 x1.22, n=3 x1.04.

2.2a Error budget: the variants
-------------------------------
The systematic errors are SHARED by every point. Adding them to each error
bar would wrongly treat them as independent scatter. Instead the whole
analysis is repeated nine times (the dict "variants", built from
"error_sources"), each time with ONE source moved by -1 or +1 sigma:

  source          what is moved                  size     from
  dead time       tau                            5.0 us   deadtime.py
  zero offset     angle scale                    0.0060   angle_offset.py
  alignment       angle scale                    0.0637   alignment_error.py
  repeatability   angle scale                    0.0143   alignment_error.py
                                                (deg)

Alignment and repeatability are errors on where the angle scale really is,
so, like the zero offset, they move every angle by the same amount.

  - zero offset: fit error, dead time, and the difference between the
    Gaussian centre and the model-free centroid of the direct beam
    (0.0054 deg; the beam is not a true Gaussian, chi^2/dof ~ 26).
  - alignment: half the shift seen when the crystal was flipped about its
    roll axis (-0.1275 deg, the same for every line). If flipping reverses
    the misalignment, the true centre lies midway.
  - repeatability: scatter of the same peak between repeat normal scans,
    pooled over all lines (7 dof). The heated-unheated angle shift
    (0.0147 deg, temperature_error.py) is the same kind of error and the
    same size, so it is printed as a cross-check and NOT added again.

Every fit is done for every variant. For each result

  error from a source = |result(source high) - result(source low)| / 2
  total error         = sqrt(fit^2 + sum of all source errors^2)

which is the same method angle_offset.py uses for tau. report() prints
each result as   value +/- total  (fit, dead time, zero offset, ...)
and the totals are the ones shown on the figures. The figures themselves
use the nominal variant. Two further errors that are not variants are
passed to report() as "extra": the fit range of the bremsstrahlung fit
(2.4) and the thermal error on d (2.10).

A shift common to every angle cancels in anything that is a DIFFERENCE of
two angles in the same scan, such as the doublet separation (2.9): there
the alignment, repeatability and zero-offset errors all come out as 0.

2.3  remove_offset(scan, peak_low, peak_high)
---------------------------------------------
Puts each dedicated scan on the same vertical level as the full scan. It
  1. picks the points outside the peak window [peak_low, peak_high];
  2. linearly interpolates the full scan onto the same angles (np.interp);
  3. takes the mean difference (dedicated - full) over those points;
  4. subtracts that constant from the whole dedicated scan.
It returns the shifted rates and the offset.

This is used for Figure 1 ONLY. The next stage fits its own baseline under
each peak, which absorbs any constant, so the peak fits use the corrected
scans without this shift.

Caveat: the n=2 and n=3 dedicated scans are about 7x more intense than the
full scan (for example the n=2 peak is 1090/s against 169/s), so the true
relationship is a scale factor, not an offset. The reason is the tube
current: 1.0 mA for n=2 and n=3 against 0.12 mA for the full scan (2.1).

of axes, with error bars.

2.4  Checkmark 1 - bremsstrahlung fit and tube voltage
------------------------------------------------------
Step A: remove the peaks.
    peak_windows lists the angle range of each characteristic line (K-beta
    and K-alpha at n=1, 2, 3, plus a small line at 15.2-15.6 deg). Points
    inside any window are thrown away. What remains (angle_bg, rate_bg)
    should be bremsstrahlung only.

Step B: convert angle to wavelength with Bragg's law, n=1:
    wavelength_bg = 2 d sin(theta).

Step C: the model, kramers(wavelength, A, lambda_min, B):

    I(lambda) = A (lambda/lambda_min - 1) / lambda^2 * exp(-B (lambda/100 pm)^3)
                for lambda > lambda_min, and 0 below it.

    - (lambda/lambda_min - 1)/lambda^2 is Kramers' law, the theoretical
      bremsstrahlung spectrum in photons per unit wavelength. A GM tube
      counts photons, so this is the right form.
    - It is zero at lambda_min (Duane-Hunt), rises, peaks and falls.
    - Kramers' law alone predicts too much at long wavelength, because
      long-wavelength X-rays are absorbed in the tube window, the air and
      the detector window. Photoelectric absorption goes roughly as
      lambda^3, hence exp(-B lambda^3). In practice B also soaks up other
      smooth wavelength effects (crystal reflectivity, detector efficiency,
      the d(lambda)/d(theta) factor), so treat it as an empirical
      correction.
    - Free parameters: A (overall scale), lambda_min, B.

Step D: weighted fit, fit_background(scan).
    Uses the dead-time-corrected rates and errors from 2.2 (1 s per step)
    and the zero-offset-corrected angles. It goes through weighted_fit
    (2.2): chi^2/dof = 1.84, so the fit errors are scaled up by 1.36. It
    is run for every variant (2.2a).

Step D2: fit-range check.
    The same fit to the background below 10 deg only, near the onset,
    where the model's simplifications (the empirical absorption term, and
    no 2nd-order bremsstrahlung, which starts at ~7 deg) matter least. It
    gives lambda_min = 35.19 pm (U = 35.23 kV) against 34.60 pm for the
    full range. The WHOLE change is taken as a "fit range" systematic. It
    is about as large as the alignment error, and Figure 2 shows why: the
    model undershoots the data around 5-10 deg.

Step E: Duane-Hunt.
    voltage = 1239.84 / lambda_min. Its fit error is propagated from the
    error on lambda_min; its other errors come from the variants and the
    fit-range check.

    Angle errors matter most here: at the onset (~3.6 deg) a 0.062 deg
    shift moves lambda_min by ~0.6 pm (1.7%), so the zero offset raises U
    from 35.24 to 35.83 kV. By the same token the 0.064 deg alignment error
    is worth 0.63 kV. The thermal error on d is worth only 0.003 kV.

FIGURE 2: background points with error bars and the Kramers fit.

2.5  Checkmark 2 - isolating each K-alpha peak
----------------------------------------------
subtract_background(scan, popt_kramers, left_flank, right_flank)

  Run for every variant, each with its own Kramers fit. Crop and flank
  windows are applied to the measured angle (2.2); the Kramers curve and
  the line are evaluated on the true angle. Results: peaks[variant].

  1. CROP the scan to [left_flank start, right_flank end]. This drops
     neighbouring features, such as the small line at ~15.4 deg next to
     the n=1 peak.
  2. SUBTRACT the Kramers fit from 2.4 (it matters only at n=1; by 33 and
     55 deg it is ~0).
  3. FIT A STRAIGHT LINE (np.polyfit, degree 1) to the flanks only: the
     flat regions either side of the peak. Then subtract it. This removes
     any leftover offset or tilt without touching the peak shape.
  4. Return the cropped angles, the baseline-free rates, the cropped raw
     weights and the slope of the line.

  Flanks used (deg):   n=1: 15.7-15.8 and 16.4-16.5
                       n=2: 32.3-32.9 and 33.8-35.5
                       n=3: 53.5-54.5 and 55.7-56.5

  After cropping, n=1 has 9 points, n=2 has 33 and n=3 has 31.

Makima spline (Akima1DInterpolator, method="makima"):
    A smooth curve through every point, FOR DISPLAY ONLY (see 1.6). It
    was chosen because:
      - a plain cubic spline overshoots and "rings" where a sharp peak
        meets a flat base;
      - PCHIP cannot put a maximum anywhere except at a data point;
      - makima avoids most overshoot and lets the maximum fall between
        points.

FIGURE 3: the three baseline-free peaks with their splines.

2.6  Single-Gaussian fit to each peak
-------------------------------------
    gaussian(x, amplitude, mean, sigma)

  - Fit to the measured points, weighted by the raw-count sigmas, through
    weighted_fit (2.2). chi^2/dof is printed for every peak fit.
  - Starting guess: height = highest point, centre = angle of the highest
    point, width = 0.1 deg.
  - Prints amplitude, mean and sigma with the full error budget (2.2a).
    The angle errors (zero offset, alignment, repeatability) go straight
    onto every fitted centre. Alignment (0.064 deg) is by far the largest,
    10-60x the fit error. Dead time changes the heights (and slightly the
    widths) but not the centres.

What the result means: the peak is really the K-alpha1 + K-alpha2 doublet,
so one Gaussian gives the intensity-weighted CENTRE of the pair. Compare it
with the weighted mean wavelength (2 x 154.06 + 154.44)/3 = 154.18 pm, not
with K-alpha1 alone. The fitted width is inflated by the splitting,
especially at n=3.

FIGURE 4: data, spline and single-Gaussian fit.

2.7  Free two-Gaussian fit
--------------------------
    two_gaussians(x, A1, mu1, sigma1, A2, mu2, sigma2)

  - All six parameters are free. Fit to the measured points with the
    raw-count weights.
  - Starting guesses use the physics:
        split = n * (154.44 - 154.06) / (2 d cos(theta))   (from 1.3)
        K-alpha1 at (centre - split/3), K-alpha2 at (centre + 2 split/3).
    With a 2:1 intensity ratio the centroid sits one third of the way from
    K-alpha1 to K-alpha2, which is why the thirds appear.
  - Bounds: heights and widths >= 0, centres inside the scanned range.

This is an UNCONSTRAINED comparison fit. With nothing tying the two
Gaussians to the physics, it often gives unphysical answers: K-alpha2
taller than K-alpha1, or one width narrower than the instrument can
produce. Those failures are what motivate the constrained fit next.
At n=2 the second Gaussian is not constrained at all: with the
corrections it shrinks to A2 ~ 7/s off to one side, and without them its
partner's centre error was +/-78 deg. Do not quote the n=2 free fit.

FIGURE 5: data, spline, both Gaussians and their sum, with a parameter box.

2.8  Two-Gaussian fit with fixed intensity ratio and shared width
-----------------------------------------------------------------
    two_gaussians_fixed_ratio(x, A1, mu1, mu2, sigma)
        = gaussian(x, A1, mu1, sigma) + gaussian(x, 0.518*A1, mu2, sigma)

Two physical constraints are built in:

  (1) SHARED WIDTH. Both lines are broadened by the same instrument at
      almost the same angle, so they must have the same sigma.

  (2) FIXED INTENSITY RATIO I2/I1 = 0.518. Intensity is AREA, A sigma
      sqrt(2 pi) (see 1.7). Because the widths are equal, the area ratio
      equals the height ratio, so setting A2 = 0.518 A1 really does fix
      the intensity ratio. (If each line had its own sigma, a fixed height
      ratio would NOT fix the intensity: the fit could change Kalpha2's area
      by making it wider or narrower.)

  Free parameters: A1, mu1, mu2, sigma (4 in all). The fit is to the
  measured points with the raw-count weights. The result is expanded back
  to six numbers [A1, mu1, sigma, 0.518 A1, mu2, sigma] so it plots in
  the same way as 2.7.

FIGURE 6: as Figure 5, for the constrained fit.

2.9  Doublet separation
-----------------------
Prints delta_mu = mu2 - mu1 (K-alpha2 centre minus K-alpha1 centre) from
the constrained fit, for each order, next to the prediction from 1.3. Its
fit error includes the correlation between the two means:
sqrt(var(mu1) + var(mu2) - 2 cov(mu1, mu2)). A constant angle shift
(zero offset, alignment, repeatability) moves both means equally, so it
cancels in delta_mu. That makes the separation the cleanest result here:
only the fit error is left.

2.10 K-alpha1 wavelength from each order
----------------------------------------
    lambda = 2 d sin(mu1) / n      (mu1 from the constrained fit, 2.8)

compared with the reference 154.06 pm. Its errors come from the variants
like everything else, plus a "thermal" error: the lab temperature is
uncertain by sigma_T (2 K, ASSUMED in temperature_error.py), so d is
uncertain by alpha sigma_T = 8e-5 of itself, and so is lambda.

An angle error delta_theta changes lambda by lambda cot(theta) delta_theta,
so the same angle error hurts n=1 (cot = 3.5) five times more than n=3
(cot = 0.7). n=3 gives the best wavelength.

The three wavelengths share the same alignment and repeatability errors
(both are angle shifts common to every scan), so their errors are
CORRELATED. Do not average them as if they were independent; quote n=3.

1. ANGLE CALIBRATION. The direct-beam zero offset (0.062 deg) is now
   removed, but the K-alpha1 peaks still sit above Bragg's law by +0.16,
   +0.16 and +0.04 deg at n = 1, 2, 3. What is left is not constant, so a
   single zero offset cannot explain it. Possible causes: the crystal's own
   zero (its surface not parallel to the beam at theta = 0), which the
   direct-beam scan without the crystal cannot see; and whether the
   direct-beam angle beta is the crystal angle theta or the detector angle
   2 theta (if it is 2 theta, the theta offset is half, 0.031 deg).
   Calibrating against the known Cu lines would resolve this.
   With the alignment error included, the n=1 and n=2 residuals are
   2.5 sigma and n=3 is 0.6 sigma, so this is no longer a gross
   disagreement, but n=1 and n=2 are still worth explaining.

2. DEAD TIME. Done: non-paralysable correction with tau = 164.5 +/- 5.0
   us from deadtime.py (2.2). The paralysable model (tau = 110 us) fits
   the current scan equally well and has not been tried here. At ~1000/s
   it would correct by x1.15 instead of x1.22, so it only matters for the
   peak HEIGHTS (~6%), not for any angle, wavelength or U.

3. TUNGSTEN LINES NOT MASKED. The "small line" at ~15.4 deg is most likely
   W L-alpha1 (147.6 pm), from filament tungsten deposited on the anode.
   Other W lines (~11.5 deg, ~13.0-13.5 deg) and the 2nd- and 3rd-order
   images of W L-alpha (~31.8-31.9 deg and ~51.8-51.9 deg) are still
   inside the Kramers background fit, and contribute to chi^2_nu = 1.85.

4. HIGHER-ORDER BREMSSTRAHLUNG. Above ~7 deg the detector also receives
   lambda/2 (2nd order) of the continuum. The model includes only n=1.
   Including n=2 moves lambda_min by up to ~1.5%.

5. SHARP CUT-OFF. The model switches on abruptly at lambda_min, but the
   instrument resolution (sigma ~0.085 deg) smears the real edge. Near
   3.6 deg that is ~2.4% of lambda.

6. OFFSET VERSUS SCALE (2.3). The dedicated scans differ from the full scan
   by a factor (~7x at n=2 and n=3), not a constant. Only Figure 1 is
   affected. Cause now known: 1.0 mA against 0.12 mA (2.1). Dividing each
   scan by its current would put them all on one scale.

7. n=1 DOUBLET UNRESOLVED. The 0.040 deg splitting is below the 0.1 deg
   step and well inside the peak width, so mu2 - mu1 at n=1 is not
   meaningful. Option: fix mu2 = mu1 + predicted splitting for n=1.

8. UNCERTAINTIES. Now combined (2.2a): fit, dead time, zero offset
   (including its shape error), alignment, repeatability, the
   bremsstrahlung fit range and the thermal error on d. The gate times
   are read from the .xry files. Fit errors are scaled up when
   chi^2/dof > 1.
   Not separate errors on purpose:
     - The 0.1 deg step size. A fitted centre is not limited to the grid,
       and any wobble in where the goniometer stops is already in the
       repeatability.
     - The heated-unheated angle shift (temperature_error.py): the same
       kind of error as repeatability, and the same size, so it would be
       counted twice.
     - The fixed intensity ratio 0.518. Checked: changing it to 0.50 or
       0.53 moves mu1 by <= 0.0015 deg and the n=3 separation by
       <= 0.0004 deg, which is negligible next to the other errors.
